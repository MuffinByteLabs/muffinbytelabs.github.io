Ask what a PCB design costs and the usual answer is "it depends". That's true, and it doesn't help you plan. So here are numbers instead: the hours typical boards take, what that comes to at my rate of $30 an hour, and what pushes a project up or down its range.

These are the same ranges the [design cost estimator](/tools/pcb-design-cost-calculator) uses. They are typical figures for typical boards, not a quote. A real project gets a fixed price in writing once there is a brief.

## Where the hours go

A new board goes through the same stages every time, from idea to factory files:

1. **Architecture and parts.** What the board has to do becomes a block diagram, then real parts: in stock, inside the budget, and small enough to fit.
2. **Schematic.** Every connection drawn, plus the supporting parts each chip's datasheet asks for: decoupling, pull-ups, protection, test points.
3. **Footprints.** Every part's pad pattern is checked against its datasheet, or drawn if it doesn't exist yet.
4. **Placement.** Where each part sits. This stage decides how hard everything after it will be, so it deserves more time than it seems to need.
5. **Routing.** The copper itself: power first, then the critical signals, then the rest.
6. **Checks and outputs.** The rule checks and a review against the schematic and the enclosure. Then the files the factory needs: Gerbers, drill files, the bill of materials and the placement file.

Skipping a stage doesn't remove its cost. It moves the cost to the second batch of boards.

## Typical hours and prices

These figures are for a new two-layer board, from idea to factory files:

- **Small**, up to about 40 parts: one main chip, a connector or two, simple power. 14 to 28 hours, **$400 to $850**.
- **Medium**, about 40 to 120 parts: a microcontroller with USB, power and a few sensors. 30 to 55 hours, **$900 to $1,650**.
- **Large**, about 120 to 250 parts: several power rails, isolated inputs and outputs, many interfaces. 55 to 100 hours, **$1,650 to $3,000**.
- **Dense**, over 250 parts, fine-pitch or BGA chips, or a very tight outline. 90 to 160 hours, **$2,700 to $4,800**.

If the schematic is already finished, layout alone takes about 60 percent of those hours. A design review before you order is a written report on a design that already exists. It runs from $100 to $200 for a small board up to $500 to $800 for a dense one.

## What adds to it

Four layers add about 10 percent, and six or more add about 25 percent. More layers make routing easier, but the [stack-up](/tools/pcb-glossary#stack-up) has to be planned and the planes checked. Boards that need more layers also tend to carry denser parts.

Some features add hours of their own, whatever the size of the board:

- **USB, Ethernet or other fast signals:** 3 to 6 hours, for the pair routing, the impedance and the return paths.
- **A radio,** such as Wi-Fi, Bluetooth or LoRa: 4 to 8 hours. That covers the antenna's keep-out zone, its place on the board edge, and the feed line if the antenna is a separate part.
- **High current or power conversion:** 4 to 8 hours, for copper sizing, the switching loop and heat.
- **Isolation, mains or high voltage:** 6 to 12 hours, for the barrier and its creepage and clearance distances.
- **A tight enclosure or a fixed outline:** 3 to 6 hours, for the back-and-forth with the mechanical drawing.

Take an ESP32-S3 board with USB-C and a handful of sensors, on four layers. It is a medium board with fast signals and a radio. That comes to 40 to 75 hours, or **$1,200 to $2,250**.

## What moves a project within its range

Where a project lands in its range mostly depends on how much is still undecided when the work starts.

- **A clear brief.** Known parts, a known enclosure and known connectors put a project at the low end. "We'll work it out as we go" puts it at the top, or makes it hourly.
- **Changes after layout starts.** Moving a connector on a routed board can undo a day of work. Settle the mechanical facts first: the outline, the mounting holes and which way each connector faces.
- **Parts you can actually buy.** If a part goes out of stock in the middle of the design, it needs a new footprint, often a new circuit around it, and sometimes a new placement. Parts with several sources, in stock at your assembler, protect the schedule as much as the price.
- **Feedback in one list.** Comments gathered into one list after each stage cost far less than a stream of single changes.
- **The deliverables.** A 3D model for the enclosure designer, assembly drawings, a production panel and a test procedure are each real work. Say up front which ones you need.

## Costs that aren't design

In a hardware budget, the design is often not the biggest line:

- **Prototype boards.** A handful of bare two-layer boards from a low-cost fab is cheap. Four layers, assembly and parts each add to that, and fast shipping can cost more than the boards themselves. Get a quote from your fab while the design is under way, not after it.
- **A second revision.** If the first boards have a fault, fixing it costs another round of fabrication, assembly, parts and shipping, plus the weeks that takes. That is the case for a design review before the first order: it costs a fraction of a second revision.
- **Certification.** Anything sold with a radio, or into a regulated market, needs testing. A pre-certified radio module avoids the most expensive part of that. The finished product usually still needs emissions testing of its own. Budget for it early, and ask a test lab about their lead time.
